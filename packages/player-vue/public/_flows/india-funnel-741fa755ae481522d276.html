The India funnel: one flow, all nine Indian-language landing pages

The flow as Tom ruled it, 8 September 2026.

Unlisted page, shareable by URL. Not linked from anywhere and not indexed.

This is the same flow for every one of the nine Indian-language landing pages — Hindi, Urdu, Bengali, Marathi, Gujarati, Punjabi, Tamil, Telugu and Kannada. Hindi is shown throughout as the worked example; read every Hindi screen below as "the learner’s own language".

Each step below has its words on the left and its own screen on the right. Every screen is a real capture of the app as it is today, except where a frame is labelled a mock-up — two screens of this flow do not exist yet, and those two are drawn rather than photographed, dashed and badged so they cannot be mistaken for the real thing.

Nobody is asked for anything before the thing that makes the ask reasonable exists. Play costs nothing. The account arrives when there is progress worth keeping. The install arrives when there is something worth buying. Money arrives last, in the app.

Where each cost arrives

  • NothingStages 1 and 2. Play, in the page, with no account. Costs the visitor nothing, not even a name.
  • An accountStage 3. Asked for when there is progress worth keeping. The reason is progress, not the store.
  • An installStages 4 and 5. Asked for at the padlock, once they have played enough to want to keep it.
  • MoneyStage 6. ₹299 a month, through Play billing, inside the app.

The stages, step by screen

Step 1

The language landing page, with the app embedded

Each language has its own landing page on the marketing site, and every one of the nine works the same way. Hindi is shown here. The marketing site carries the lesson player as a web component inside the page. Not an iframe, not a deep link. Embedded, so back, scroll, the keyboard and the safe area all belong to one document.

There is one door on that page and it is Play. No account. No store link. No download button. No store badge. Nothing to type.

Tom, on why: "I don't think people are going to download an app they haven't played with. Not these days." And on why the store link does not belong at the top: "there is a big delay between clicking on the link and just being able to play it, which they don't even need an account for."

The copy line that nothing needs installing stays, and stays true. It is true of the play. It was only ever wrong about the purchase, and that is fixed by adding the upgrade story lower down the page, not by softening the line.

Step 2

The web trial, still no account

The trial is the web app in the learner’s own language, Hindi here, and it is gate-free. No card, no email, no sign-up.

The player the visitor taps in the page is the same component as the app itself, so the trial is not a teaser of the product — it is the product, running.

Step 3

Progress worth keeping triggers create-account

The account arrives when there is something worth keeping, and that something is progress. The reason for the account is not that the store needs one. It is that progress needs one.

Identity is Google sign-in preferred, email as fallback. Google is preferred because on Android the person already has a Google account, Play billing will use it whatever you do, it requires no typing in Hindi or English, and, unlike Apple's relay address or a passwordless magic link, it returns a real, writable email address.

Step 4

The padlock, and the only place the Play Store appears

The store link appears once, at the padlock, after they have played enough to want to keep it.

The link carries two things in the Play install referrer string: the course code, so the Android app opens in the right language on first launch with no language question asked, and a one-time, short-lived account token bound to the web account, so the app signs them in silently the first time it opens. One mechanism, both doors.

Read the referrer once, early, on first launch, and store what you found. Fall back to one tap on a language if the referrer is empty. A sideload or another store hands the app nothing, and India is Play, so that is a corner.

This is deferred deep linking. It is what removes the manual sign-in step and guarantees the purchase attaches to the right account.

This screen does not exist yet, so the picture that goes with it is a drawing of the ruled step rather than a capture of a built one.

Step 5

The Android app opens and reads the referrer

The Android app is a wrap of this same web app, so what it shows on first open is what the web app shows on first open. The referrer is what tells it which course and which account it is opening for.

Read it once, on first launch, and store what you found. With no referrer to read, the app has to guess — and the capture that goes with this step shows exactly what that guess looks like.

Step 6

In-app purchase through Play

Play billing is wired, and it offers UPI. Tom saw it live on a call to India: the purchase sheet came up over the app and UPI was there as a payment option. That is observed behaviour, not a plan.

There is no photograph of it here because a Play purchase sheet needs a signed-in Android device with a Play account on it. A headless browser cannot raise one, so the frame that goes with this step is a drawing of the sheet Tom saw rather than a capture of it. The gap is the camera, not the feature.

Google's rule is satisfied: the web sends people to the store, the app sells in-app, and nothing inside the app links out to a web till.

What is not built on this leg, and should not be read as built:

  • The server-side join of the Play purchase token to the SSi account. Without it a purchase is a Play fact rather than an entitlement on the learner's account.
  • The ₹299-a-month product itself. What was seen on the call was a purchase of the existing listing at its existing fee, which is what proves the billing plumbing and the UPI option — it does not prove the price we intend to charge.

Whether anything sits between those two — a partly-configured subscription product, a half-written webhook — is not something this page can stand behind either way, and it is worth an hour of somebody reading the Play console and the API before it is asserted in either direction.

Institutions are a separate rail

Invoice or card through Paddle, never in-app. It leaves the same landing page, but it is a parallel rail rather than a branch of the consumer funnel.

How we identify someone who writes to support

Tom's objection, verbatim: "Identifying them is a nightmare now. How can we know who they are when or if they contact support? This is the utter ball ache we have right now with all these super easy sign in with methods."

The answer: the lookup key is a property of the account, shown to the customer. It is never a property of how they signed in. Concretely, three things, attached to the account stage and the purchase stage:

  • A short code on the account screen, with the line quote this if you write to us.
  • A contact-support affordance inside the app that sends the account id, so the artefact declares who they are rather than the person having to remember.
  • For a paying customer, the Play order number on their receipt. That resolves them with no identity question at all, because the server stores the purchase token against the account anyway.

Undecided — Tom's

Which Android package id the app ships under: the existing com.automagic.a3f listing, which around fifty thousand Indian users already have installed, or a fresh one.

It is permanent and irreversible, and it is the one piece of this flow that is not detectable at the moment of violation. Nothing will tell you a fresh id was the wrong call until someone who already has the app installed cannot find their subscription.

It is also the only thing that can make the referrer link land in the wrong app, so it blocks end-to-end testing of stages 4 and 5.

Footnote on the store listing: the Play store page itself is shown in the phone's locale. The referrer fixes the app's language, but not the store page. Nine localised store listings are copy work that has not been done, so a Hindi visitor would currently read an English store page between two Hindi screens.
