01 — Why listening

Once you can produce, listening does the rest.

There's a tipping point. Before it, you build capacity. After it, you widen it.

Early on, learning is hard productive work — prompt, pause, attempt, hear it back. That builds productive capacity: the ability to say things. But once you have that capacity, you no longer need intensive productive effort on every new item. New vocabulary can ride in on comprehensible input — listening you mostly understand, where the unknown is carried by the known around it.

"I don't really need all that much productive work with new items now — I've done a lot of that, and I now have a lot of productive capacity. Wider listening is the key."— Aran Jones, on finishing Irish

The pod is how we deliver that wider listening: natural, scene-based conversation, not restricted to known words, tuned so the learner can follow without text. Showing text would remove the very effort that makes it stick — so by default there's none.

02 — The shape of a pod

A day, in scenes.

Greetings, a coffee, an aperitif, the restaurant, the market, the hotel, the pharmacy, directions, a taxi.

Pod 0 walks one character through an ordinary day. Each scene is a short, natural exchange between two speakers; each row is one line of dialogue and its plain translation. That's all the author writes — the target sentence and its English. Everything else (the part-by-part breakdown, the speeds, when English appears and when it stops) is produced downstream and decided by the engine.

The three live pods — Spanish, French, Croatian — share this scene spine exactly, which is why they're directly comparable. Where they differ is how finely a line is cut, and that's the subject of figures B and C.

03 — The stage-through

One sentence, maturing.

The same line, heard again across sessions — but never the same way twice.

When a sentence first appears, it's slow, broken down, and propped up with English. Each time it comes back, it loses a little support and gains a little speed — until eventually it's just the target, at full pace, with no scaffolding at all. 04 — Breaking a line down

Two ways to make a sentence followable.

Cut it into pieces — or keep it whole and explain the pieces once.

A long line needs to be made comprehensible. There are two strategies, and our three pods are split across them. Chunking slices the sentence into atomic pieces, each its own row, each practised on its own. The explainer keeps the sentence whole and, the first time it appears, plays an auto-generated part-by-part gloss in Tom's voice — "X means Y, A means B" — then never again; after that, just the translation.

The explainer isn't authored — Haiku generates it from the sentence and its translation, then it's rendered to audio. Croatian was chunked instead, so it has no explainers at all. Toggle the same French line both ways:

05 — The lap that grows

The pod gets longer as you go.

Sentence n joins on the n-th lap. Everything older has moved on ahead of it.

A pod lap isn't fixed. On each lap a new sentence is introduced at stage 1, while every earlier sentence has aged forward into later stages. So a single lap is a cross-section of the whole journey at once — the newest line getting its breakdown, the oldest lines flashing past at 2×. 06 — The open question

Bigger sentences, carried by a better breakdown.

Built to be argued with.

The working hypothesis: bigger sentences are better — more natural, more rhythm, more real comprehensible input — provided the breakdown does enough work the first time. That means leaning on the explainer model (Spanish / French) rather than chunking everything small (Croatian), and making the explainer genuinely good. Croatian would come back to whole sentences and get an explainer pass it has never had.

What we'd settle with Aran

  1. Granularity. Whole-sentence + explainer as the default, chunking only where a line truly won't sit? Or keep chunking for some scenes?
  2. Once vs first stage. Today the breakdown plays through the whole first stage (~5 laps). Should it be literally once, then translation — closer to "first time, then just the translation"?
  3. Explainer quality bar. If sentences get bigger, the gloss carries more load. What's good enough — and does it want Tom's voice throughout, or only the gloss?
  4. Translation cadence. How long should plain English persist after the breakdown drops, before the target stands on its own?
