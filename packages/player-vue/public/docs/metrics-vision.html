01 — The premise

The method already works. We are adding finesse, not replacing it.

Seventeen years of learners give us a ground truth almost no platform has.

Across thousands of learners, SSi has observed two anchors: at roughly 30 hours of in-app practice people get confidently into conversations; at roughly 100 hours they are genuinely conversational, around B1. That is empirical fact — but it is flat: the same number for everyone, with no per-learner finesse and no external cross-check.

This paper describes the layer that adds the finesse while preserving everything the method already does. The objective never changes, and it is not "course coverage" or "vocabulary count":

Get people into conversations — as understandable as possible, as quickly as possible — where they finesse their competence.

Everything below is downstream of that one sentence. When a metric and the objective disagree, the metric is wrong.

02 — Rate of change, not level

The signal is the second derivative.

The differential of the difference. Everything is about rate of change.

The absolute value of a metric carries little information. A learner who consistently takes 2.5× as long as the model voice — but says things nicely — is simply where they are. What matters is change, and specifically the rate of change of the rate of change.

Differentiate any signal twice and two things vanish on their own: the level (a constant offset) and the steady slope (someone improving at an even pace). What is left is only the inflection — the moment the trend itself turns. That is exactly the discrimination we want, and it is baseline-free: no need to store a personal baseline to subtract.

03 — Contextual difficulty

Difficulty is an interaction. Adaptation spends a budget.

Not "is this phrase hard?" but "is this hard for this learner, now?"

Difficulty is not a property of a phrase — it is a property of (this learner × this unit), and it moves as their map fills in. We read it by running the acceleration sensor locally: per LEGO, per word, and especially per boundary — the join between units, where retrieval-while-still-producing tends to catch people.

A session is a finite practice budget. The engine's real lever is not how hard to push a single item but how to spend that budget across three moves:

  • Consolidate — reinforce what they are already good at.
  • Defer — reduce a hard item now; let it rest until its surrounding context is richer.
  • Drill — increase a hard item.

The SSi lean, against the mainstream instinct: default to consolidate and defer; reserve drill for the structurally critical few.

Why? A hard item is usually blocked by a thin surrounding map, not by lack of exposure to itself. Grinding it in isolation produces brittle memorisation and risks the learner drowning. Consolidating strengths builds the confidence that keeps them in the session (the bored wall is affective as much as cognitive) and thickens the semantic map that makes the deferred item land almost for free on return. The whole engine is a controller with one job: keep the learner in the channel between drowning and boredom, where rate of progress is highest — and it does it invisibly, by nudging repetition counts and the pause multiplier before a struggle becomes a crash.

04 — Measure the objective

Never a convenient proxy.

The same lesson has bitten three times.

Every time we reach for an off-the-shelf measure, it mismeasures exactly what makes SSi work:

  • No ASR — speech recognition scores whether a machine can guess your words. That is not fluency. Fluency is timing, rhythm, prosody, plausibility.
  • Not drill-the-weakness — the Anki instinct optimises coverage, not rate of progress toward conversation.
  • Not frequency — and this is the subtle one. The meta-communication layer we teach first ("I'm not sure how to say…", "can you say that again", "I want to try") is rare in any corpus, but it is the highest-impact material, because it is what lets a learner stay in a conversation and negotiate. Corpus frequency would rank it near the bottom.

So "how critical is this LEGO?" is answered not by frequency but by conversational impact — and the method already encodes that, for free, in the order it introduces things. Early = high impact, by design. A hard early-layer LEGO therefore resists deferral, even though it is "rare". And the better-simpler-cheaper corollary: don't build a richer signal before the thing that consumes it exists — use introduction order now; earn the fancier version only if the data shows it failing.

05 — The comparison model

One chart. Two clocks. A whole dashboard.

"Is this learner / class / school on track?" — answered by comparison, shown one way.

Any two things can be compared, and the combinatorics explode quickly. The discipline is to notice it is really one display: a growth curve, drawn against a comparison band. What changes is only which entity, which band, and — crucially — which clock is on the x-axis.

Two clocks tell different truths. In-app minutes isolates efficiency — progress per hour practised; the method's own claim lives here. Wall-clock days isolates efficiency × intensity — the real-world outcome. A class that takes Irish seriously does not learn better per hour; it crams more hours into fewer days, so it reaches usability in fewer weeks. The gap between the two clocks is the intensity signal.

06 — Sovereignty

Compare to an aggregate. Never to a named peer.

A class is a learner in its own right — it plays as a class — not an average of its pupils.

Every comparison is entity → the average of its level, or entity → a collective it belongs to. Never entity → a singled-out peer. An individual can see how they sit against their class, or against the average of the school; never against another named child. A class compares to the average of classes in the school; never to another class. And so on, all the way up. This protects the sovereignty of every entity — no league tables, no naming and shaming.

07 — Three reference frames

Self, group, world.

Your three priorities are really three frames of comparison.

The same coordinate, read against three different references — and each maps to one of the three things we want to do, in order.

01

Adapt

relative to self

The engine keeps each learner in their own flow. The curvature lens — did this learner change? It ignores constant offsets on purpose. Invisible; the learner never feels it.

02

Surface

relative to the group

The teacher's view. Group-relative catches exactly the curvature's blind spot — the learner who is persistently behind but stable. "Who changed" + "who's off the pace", together.

03

Certify

relative to the world

An absolute, externally-anchored band — best-fit CEFR. Neither self nor group, but a calibrated comparison to everyone, with explicit confidence.

A caveat that ties them together: group-relative has a failure mode — a whole class that is collectively behind looks "fine" because everyone is off-pace together. So the group frame needs the absolute frame as a backstop (the 30/100-hour anchor now, CEFR later). All three matter.

08 — Absolute competence

CEFR as a best-fit, not a certificate.

Derivatives adapt; the level certifies. Same coordinate, opposite reading.

The honest claim, and the boundary of it: SSi measures speaking-production fluency plus course coverage. A formal CEFR exam measures four skills including spontaneous interaction. So our output is a speaking-fluency proxy aligned to CEFR-equivalent bands — a continuous screening / progress indicator, not a certification. SSi is not an exam board. For a school that is more useful than a point-in-time exam: it is always-on and trend-aware.

Two facts set the timeline. App populations are bottom-heavy, so the lower-to-mid bands calibrate years before B2+. And the school pilots are the calibration engine — they supply authority, a spread of levels, and paired external assessment. Conveniently, Welsh (National Centre for Learning Welsh), Irish (TEG) and Spanish (DELE/SIELE) all sit against CEFR-aligned frameworks already.

09 — What ships when

Behavioural first. Prosody is paced by adoption.

~26k telemetry events exist today; prosody / VAD is near 0%.

The architecture was written "prosody first". Reality is the inverse: the behavioural tier — what we infer from clicks, pauses, completion, cadence and the phase-pill self-assessment — has rich data today and can power the first dashboards and the adaptation immediately. The executional tier — prosody differentials between the learner's sound wave and the model's, once VAD matures — is the most novel part, but it grows only as fast as opt-in does.

  • Now — wire the phase-pill events; behavioural execution proxy; the "needs attention" list and group-relative comparison on data that already exists.
  • Weeks–months — prosody capture in the browser (no server round-trip, no ASR); adoption driven by methodology pages like this one.
  • Months — tutor & schools dashboards on persisted per-learner state; the comparison chart of Figure B as a real surface.
  • Months–years — population layer and CEFR calibration off the pilots.
10 — For discussion

Open questions for us.

Decisions this paper deliberately leaves open

  1. Communication layers. Do we make "conversational impact" an explicit tag in Popty, or keep leaning on introduction order as the proxy until the data forces our hand? (BSC says: order, for now.)
  2. The default comparison set. Confirm: every entity compares to its parent aggregate and to the language-wide band — and peer-vs-peer is never offered, not even to government. Agreed?
  3. Which clock is default for whom. In-app minutes for teachers/learners; wall-clock for school-leader/government framing. Does that split feel right?
  4. The k-anonymity threshold. What minimum membership makes an "average" safe — 5 learners? 3 classes? This is the one place sovereignty can leak.
  5. Pilot calibration mechanics. What exactly do the Wales/Ireland pilots hand us as external CEFR truth — teacher judgement, formal exam results, or both?

Everything here is a draft to be argued with. The companion specification — with the full schema, event types and implementation phases — lives at docs/methodology/metrics-architecture.md.
