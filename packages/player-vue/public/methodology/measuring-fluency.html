Measurement

Measuring what fluency actually is.

Most platforms measure whatever is easiest to count. We start from the other end: what is fluency, what does seventeen years of observation already tell us, and what's the most honest claim the data supports? — then measure exactly that, and nothing more convenient.

Companion: How We Listen (the microphone-level story) Read: ~5 minutes
01 · The ground truth

Two numbers, seventeen years in the making

Before any clever instrumentation, the method already has empirical anchors most platforms would kill for.

~30 hrs

of in-app practice, and learners are confidently getting into conversations. Not fluent — but the barrier to speaking has dropped.

~100 hrs

and they're genuinely conversational, at roughly B1 level — extended exchanges, unfamiliar topics, recovering from mistakes.

These are population averages observed across thousands of learners since 2009 — never promises to individuals; the variance is real. But they are ground truth that needs no external validation, and everything else in the measurement architecture is built to add what these flat averages lack: individual resolution. Is this learner ahead of the curve or quietly stalling? The average can't say. The architecture can.

Why the method produces these numbers at all: a learner who already speaks one language isn't missing a map of how language works — they have grammar, meaning, pragmatics already. What they're missing is the automatic memories connecting what they mean to how the new language says it. The SSi cycle is a high-throughput factory for exactly those memories, and the hours-to-conversation figures are simply how long the factory takes at our intensity.

02 · The picture

A coordinate, read as a curve

Every learner is a point on a two-axis plane — and the point matters far less than its trajectory.

The two axes, borrowed loosely from Olympic diving: difficulty — how far into the course you are, objective, no judgement — and execution — how closely your spoken responses match the model voice, heard as shape (timing, melody, rhythm), never as transcribed words. Far along and executing well: fluent. Far along, executing poorly: clicking through. Early but executing beautifully: thorough. Early and struggling: exactly who a teacher should go to first.

The same coordinate can be read three ways, carrying very different amounts of information:

Level

Where you are

Mostly context. A learner who consistently takes 2.5× the model's length, steadily, saying things nicely, is simply where they are. No alarm.

Velocity

Where you're heading

A mild signal — trending up or down. By the time velocity has visibly changed sign, the turn already happened.

Acceleration

The trend of the trend

The headline. The moment a trajectory starts to bend — often before the learner has consciously registered they're struggling.

There's a quiet piece of mathematics doing the work here. Differentiate any signal twice and two things vanish automatically: the constant level, and any steady slope. What survives is only the inflection — the moment the trend itself turns. So a stable-but-slow learner reads as exactly zero — no alarm, correctly, with no per-learner baseline to estimate — while a learner drifting from 2.5× to 4× over a handful of cycles lights up immediately. If 4× then settles as the new normal, the alarm falls silent again on its own.

The level is context. The acceleration is the alarm.

03 · The discipline

Measure the objective, never a convenient proxy

Every off-the-shelf measure mismeasures exactly what makes the method work.

The objective is confident interaction — as understandable as possible, as quickly as possible. Held against that, the standard tools all point the wrong way:

  • Speech recognition scores whether a machine can guess your words. Fluency is timing, melody and rhythm — and speech recognition is weakest on the long-tail languages we care most about, and biased against exactly the non-native voices we teach. We never use it.
  • Word-frequency lists rank the wrong material. The phrases that get you into a conversation and keep you there — "I'm not sure how to say…", "can you say that again?" — are rare in any corpus and priceless in the mouth. Our courses front-load by conversational impact, not frequency, which is why the ordering of a course is itself expert judgement, compiled.
  • Test scores measure a day's performance. We measure the practice itself, continuously and invisibly — no test days, no marking, no examiner needed.
04 · Levels

Discovered from the population, not imposed on it

What "what level am I?" gets as an answer — and what it never will.

Formal frameworks like CEFR assert levels from theory. We go the other way: as learners spanning beginner to fluent accumulate on the difficulty-and-execution plane, clusters emerge — and our own 30-hour and 100-hour anchors, plus a small sample of learners with known external scores, let us label those clusters after the fact. School pilots pair a learner's internal coordinate with the school's own assessment judgement; if the two agree, the method is doubly validated, and if they disagree, we've learned something worth knowing. Either result is a win, which is what makes it honest measurement rather than marketing.

  • Never a hard level. Always a best-fit band with explicit confidence: "your speaking patterns are most similar to learners around B1, and confidence will tighten as you practise."
  • Never a certificate. We measure speaking-production fluency plus course coverage, and we say so. A formal assessment measures four skills; we won't claim the other three.
  • Always "speaking-pattern similarity". That's the most the data honestly supports, so it's the most we'll ever say.

Why publish the measurement philosophy at all? Because the methodology has never been a trade secret, and a system that grades people owes them an explanation of the ruler. Being the organisation that explains its work is worth more than the mystique of not explaining it.
