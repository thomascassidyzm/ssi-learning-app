Adaptation

The invisible accompanist.

You own the big dials — skip forward, skip back, speed up. The engine does the finesse underneath, at a scale you'd never bother with manually: and if it's working, you never feel it. Like a good accompanist following a soloist, the soloist just feels in flow.

Status: design position, engine rolling out in stages Read: ~4 minutes
01 · The division of labour

Your judgement is the major dial

Adaptive systems usually fight the learner for control. Ours refuses to.

The learner already owns the big decisions in the player. Skip forward means "too easy". Skip back means "let me hear that again". Those manual controls do the heavy lifting, and the engine never competes with them — a learner's own judgement about their learning is data to respect, not noise to override.

What the engine does is everything you couldn't be bothered to do: shave a fraction of a second off a pause when your last five responses came quickly, bring a phrase you struggled with back slightly sooner, give a phrase you've clearly got a little more air. Millisecond-scale finesse, continuously, invisibly.

And a hard line with it: anything you'd notice — reordering the course, surfacing a remedial track — is never done silently. It goes to a human, as a suggestion. The engine's silent powers are bounded to the imperceptible.

02 · The objective

The channel between drowning and bored

What the finesse is actually for.

the drown wall — too hard → brittle memorisation, giving up
keep the learner here, where progress is fastest
the bored wall — too easy → attention drifts, sessions stop

Both walls end the same way — the learner leaves — but they need opposite nudges, and the trick is nudging early. The engine watches the trend of each learner's responses and acts the moment a trend starts to bend, before a developing struggle becomes a crash and before coasting becomes boredom. By the time either is obvious, you're late. (Why the bend is the earliest honest signal is the curvature argument in Measuring What Fluency Actually Is.)

03 · The lean

Consolidate, defer, drill — in that order

Against the drill-your-weakness instinct of most education software.

A practice session is a finite budget. When something turns out to be hard for you, the engine has three ways to spend the next slice of it:

Default

Consolidate

Reinforce what you're already good at around the hard thing — thicken the map it has to land in.

Usually

Defer

Rest the hard item and let the spaced-repetition schedule bring it back — deferral is timing, never abandonment.

Rarely

Drill

Push the hard item directly — reserved for the structurally critical few that block everything downstream.

The lean runs against the mainstream instinct, so here is the reasoning plainly. A hard item is usually blocked by a thin surrounding map, not by lack of exposure to itself. Grinding it in isolation buys brittle memorisation and pushes you at the drown wall. Consolidating strengths keeps you confidently in the session, and fills in the surroundings — so when the deferred item returns on its schedule, it tends to land almost for free.

Which items resist deferral? Not the frequent ones — the early ones. Our courses are sequenced by conversational impact, so how early something was introduced is itself the record of how critical it is. A hard phrase from the early layers gets held onto even if it's rare in any corpus, because it's part of what unlocks conversation itself.

And it's falsifiable

Because the measurement layer captures everything the adaptation does, the consolidate-lean is a testable bet, not a creed: as learner and school-pilot data accumulate, we can check whether defer-and-consolidate actually beats drill-the-weakness for rate of progress. Seventeen years of the method working says it will. The architecture gets to check its own pedagogy rather than assert it — and if the data ever disagrees, the data wins.
