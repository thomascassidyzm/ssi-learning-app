Teaching insight

The class is
a learner.

A learner who plays alone needs watching — has anyone looked at them this week? A class that plays together is itself a learner — track it like one. Two different questions, two different lanes. Most education dashboards fail by mixing them into one junk drawer.

Audience: teachers, school leaders, the curious Read: ~4 minutes
01 · The cut

Attention and coverage

One honest distinction does the work of a hundred dashboard widgets.

When a class runs an SSi session together — one device, one speaker, thirty voices answering at once — there is no honest way to attribute that collective sound to individual children. We refuse to pretend otherwise. So instead of faking per-pupil data out of a classroom microphone, we made the class itself a first-class learner: it has a position in the course, a record of what it has done, a rate of progress — exactly as an individual learner does.

Attention

Who needs a human?

For learners practising alone — homework, or a tutor's roster. Nobody was in the room, so someone should look. A short triage list: three names bubble up, the teacher acts, done. No charts.

Coverage

How is the class-learner doing?

For the class as a whole. Where it has reached, how fast it's moving, how much practice it's actually getting. Read by its teacher the way a tutor reads a learner — and by the leaders above, rolled up.

What a teacher does not get is a behavioural debrief of the lesson they just taught — "you replayed this, you skipped that." They were in the room. It answers a question they don't have. The system's job is to remember what they can't be expected to hold across five classes and a term, not to narrate what they just did.

02 · The coverage lane

Four numbers a leader can act on

Not a wall of analytics. A distillation.

  • Coverage — where the class has reached in the course.
  • Rate — how fast it's advancing in calendar time. The headline.
  • Dosage — active minutes per week. Flow rate, not a cumulative vanity total.
  • Efficiency — progress per active minute. Two classes at the same coverage on very different minutes are two different stories, and that contrast is exactly the leadership question.

One consequence of the rate-first framing deserves calling out. A confident teacher who says "you've got this — straight on to the next thing", often, with the class keeping up, produces a fast, healthy curve. A test-score lens would read those skips as gaps. Skipping ahead is a mastery signal, and only this framing makes it legible as one.

And because progress belongs to the class, not to whoever drove a given lesson, teacher changes never break the record. A class can change teacher, share two, take a supply cover — the curve doesn't blink.

03 · The attention lane

Where a per-pupil read is honest

Individual signals come only from individual practice.

When a pupil practises alone — homework on their own device — a per-pupil read is legitimate, because the data really is theirs. That's the only place we do it. The attention lane is a triage list, sorted by simple, explainable rules: gone quiet this week, responses suddenly slower, lots of skipping back. Alongside each name, one genuinely unusual signal: whether that learner's confidence matches their performance — because an overconfident and an underconfident pupil can have identical scores and need opposite help. (How that signal is measured — without ever recording a word anyone says — is the subject of a separate briefing.)

  • Per-pupil data comes only from solo practice. Never inferred from the collective sound of a classroom — that attribution would be fiction, and we don't ship fiction.
  • Missing data is stated, not painted over. "Maria keeps up in class but has no homework data — we can't tell how she's actually speaking." That sentence is the feature: it tells the teacher the truth and hands them the lever (encourage the homework).
  • Recommendations are graded, not bossy. "Message Megan — nine days quiet." "Look at this phrase with Huw." "Celebrate Eleri." A curated nudge, never a taskmaster.

One more thing the two lanes make possible: when the third class this week stalls on the same phrase, that's not a teaching problem in any of those classrooms — it's a course-content problem, and it routes to the course authors. Friction that follows the material across classes belongs to the material. Teachers should never wear it.
