# Pace and settings — explainer copy draft

Draft copy only. Nothing here is wired in. It replaces the block in **Why this works** currently headed *Thirty hours, spent however suits you*.

Two drafts. Same content, different way in. Pick by ear.

---

## Draft one — leads with the three paces

**Heading:** Thirty hours, spent however suits you

Thirty is the number that matters, and there are wildly different ways to spend it. Six hours a day for five days. An hour a day for a month. Five minutes a day for a year. The same thirty hours, and nothing alike to live through.

Easy and Fast sit inside that choice. Easy gives you about double the thinking time and says each phrase twice over, so the work is genuinely gentler while you are doing it. Fast asks more of you in the moment. Both go to the same place.

The catch is that easy is a bit of an illusion. Taking it gently really is the better learning, because the days in between are when it all settles, and if you can keep it up you will end up ahead. But keeping up five minutes a day for a year asks far more of you than a couple of big weekends, and for most of that year you will not feel much happening.

So if you can, start with a big stretch. A whole day of it, then an hour a day, then another whole day when it suits. The early burst is what gets you something real to show for it — a first conversation, the first thing that arrives without you reaching for it. And going fast has one plain advantage: you get there sooner, and you can start using the language sooner.

## Draft two — leads with the recommendation

**Heading:** The best way to spend thirty hours

If we had to tell you one thing about how to spend the thirty hours, it is this. Start with a big stretch, then settle into a rhythm. A whole day of it, then an hour a day, then another whole day when you can.

That is not because rushing teaches you more. It does not. Thirty hours spread thin gives your brain far more time to settle in between, and Easy hands you about double the thinking time and says each phrase twice over. Taken steadily, that really is the better learning, and we would not pretend otherwise.

It is that five minutes a day for a year is a much harder thing to actually do than it sounds, and for most of that year you would not feel much happening. A burst at the start changes that. You get a first conversation out of it, and the faith that this works on you, months before the gentle road would hand you either.

And there is one plain advantage to going fast. You get there sooner, and you can start using the language sooner.

## What changed, in plain English

**Now — two paragraphs:**

> Thirty is the number that matters. How you spread them is entirely yours. An hour a day for a month works. Six hours a day for five days works too, if you have a real burn on and the time to feed it.
>
> Five minutes a day sounds like the gentle option, and it is actually the hardest road of the lot — the most decisions to make, and the least evidence per sitting that any of it is working. If you can give it bigger stretches, give it bigger stretches.

**What it gets right and keeps:** thirty is the number, the spread is yours, five minutes a day is the hardest road, bigger stretches are better.

**What it is missing, and the draft adds:**

- **Easy and Fast are named and explained here at all.** The current block talks about pace without ever mentioning the two buttons the learner actually presses, so the settings and the spread read as unrelated things. They are the same argument.
- **The work being gentler on Easy is stated as a real fact, not implied.** Double the thinking time, each phrase twice over.
- **Why the slow road is genuinely better is now given a reason** — the days in between are when it settles — instead of the slow road being only a warning.
- **The illusion is named.** Easy is the gentler work and the harder ask. The current copy gestures at this; it never says it.
- **There is now a recommendation with a shape**: a big stretch, then a daily rhythm, then another big stretch. The current copy ends on "give it bigger stretches", which is advice without a plan.
- **Going fast gets its honest advantage stated**: you arrive sooner and can use the language sooner. Not better learning. Sooner.
- **Five minutes a day for a year** is now said as a length of time rather than as a per-sitting complaint, which is what makes the adherence point land.

Length: current block is 108 words. Draft one is 210, draft two is 185. Both are longer than their neighbours because this block is now carrying an argument the others do not, but neither is double.

## Note

**Calls I made, all as defaults — say the word and any of them flips.**

- **One block, not two.** Kept as a single block replacing the existing one, as briefed. It held together at this length, so there was no reason to split it.
- **Heading.** Draft one keeps the existing heading, which still fits — the block is still about how thirty hours get spent. Draft two changes it, because leading with the recommendation makes "spent however suits you" read like the wrong promise.
- **The three currencies are never named.** How hard the work feels, how likely you are to keep doing it, and what the brain gets from the gaps — all three are in there as plain observations about the learner's own experience, and none of them appears as a label.
- **No mountain in the words.** One echo only: *both go to the same place* in draft one, *the same thirty hours* throughout. Nothing that reads as a caption for the drawing, and both drafts stand up read alone.
- **Tom's ten-hour day became "a whole day of it".** Ten hours in learner-facing copy reads as a dare rather than an invitation, and the shape of the advice is what matters, not the number. Easy to put back if you want the number said out loud.

**One thing I think needs you.**

The bullet in *How this works* — "Fast is the standard pace. Easy gives you about double the thinking time and more repetition, for when you want longer to get the words out." — does not contradict either draft, and I have not touched it. But "for when you want longer to get the words out" now reads slightly thin next to a block arguing that Easy is the better learning. A one-word-heavier version would be: *Easy gives you about double the thinking time and says each phrase twice over.* Your call, and a separate job.
